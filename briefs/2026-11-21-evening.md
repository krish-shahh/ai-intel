## The signal

1. METR updates its time-horizon chart: coding keeps climbing, judgment lags
   What happened: The AI evaluation nonprofit METR published an update to its time-horizon plot, MIT Technology Review reports. The new report finds some AI capabilities are still improving along an exponential trajectory, but models aren't as good at tasks that need judgment as they are at coding. MIT Technology Review calls it "the most misunderstood graph in AI."
   Why it matters: The time-horizon chart is often cited as evidence that agents will soon handle long autonomous work. A split between coding and judgment tasks would mean that headline trend says more about coding agents than about agents in general. The collected summary doesn't give the new numbers, the models tested, or the task sets, and it doesn't say whether the work came under the Tier 3 access to Anthropic models on the watchlist, so I'm not linking the two.
   Confidence: Reported (single outlet describing the evaluator's report)

3. Two million planted secrets show user data leaking through the AI supply chain
   What happened: Ars Technica reports on a researcher who planted 2 million "canaries" (prompts carrying unique, trackable secrets) into the AI supply chain. Per the summary, the test "proves that data doesn't stay private," and the headline says a "privacy disaster surfaced."
   Why it matters: Prompts pass through more than the model vendor: routers, wrappers, logging tools, and resellers. If secrets in prompts turn up somewhere else later, prompt data can't be treated as confidential just because the main provider's terms say so. The summary doesn't say where the canaries resurfaced, which services were involved, or how often, so I can't name any company here.
   Confidence: Reported (single outlet describing one researcher's test)
   Sources: [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/11/a-leaky-ai-supply-chain-is-exposing-secret-user-data-at-an-alarming-rate/) (independent)

The Verge notes this comes as states and cities around the country restrict new applications to build them.
   Why it matters: This pushes against the local backlash already on the watchlist. The federal message is to build here, while towns like Red Oak, Texas push back on new sites.
