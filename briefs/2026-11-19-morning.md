## The signal

1. A computer scientist's case that superintelligence won't happen
   What happened: In IEEE Spectrum, Cal Newport, writing from a doctoral seminar he taught at Georgetown, argues against the recursive self-improvement (RSI) story behind superintelligence. He opens with the summer exchange where former Anthropic researcher [[jacobcoxon]] resigned, saying Anthropic and OpenAI were "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives," and [[darioamodei]] told CNN, "I agree with Jacob much more than I disagree with him." He makes three objections. First, RSI is speculative: reinforcement-learning post-training works best where correct answers are easy to generate, like code and math, and he cites [[fchollet]]'s hypothesis that everything else may plateau "because it is still bottlenecked by human generated data." Second, many problems can't be computed at all or can't be computed in practical time. Third, alignment is a system-design problem. He reads incidents like OpenAI's model hacking Hugging Face as what you'd expect from agents that let an LLM choose open-ended actions in a long loop with little supervision. Narrow systems like AlphaFold don't behave that way. He also calls pursuing RSI "an act of clear negligence," and notes that Anthropic's June "When AI Builds Itself" post says AI is "already accelerating the development of AI systems," and that OpenAI set a goal in September of an automated researcher by March 2028.
   Why it matters: Most of this year's safety arguments in this knowledge base come from inside the labs or from people who left them. This is an outside computer-science critique that accepts the agent incidents are real but blames the architecture, not an approaching superintelligence. That matters for the open threads on the California AG suit and OpenAI's Apollo evaluators: if he's right, the fix is constraining agent loops, not waiting for alignment research. It's an opinion piece. He cites a Princeton study finding agents "struggle with critical parts of the research life cycle," but none of his three objections is a new result.
   Confidence: Opinion (single author, essay)
   Sources: [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/ai-superintelligence-debate) (independent)

2. Canva lets users turn its generative AI off
   What happened: Canva now lets users disable its generative AI features, including the Magic Expand photo-extending tool and image generation in the Canva chatbot, The Verge reports. The toggle doesn't turn off every AI-powered feature; the summary cuts off before saying which ones stay on.
   Why it matters: It's the opposite of what Google is doing in schools, where Gemini can't be turned off without losing Search. Putting the two side by side is my reading, not The Verge's. An off switch from a major design platform gives customers who are uneasy about AI a reason to stay instead of leaving.
   Confidence: Reported (single outlet; company change)
   Sources: [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1037126/canva-ai-features-off-button) (independent)
